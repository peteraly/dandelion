# Dandelion — Build Prompt J: the problem first, every stakeholder, and whether the incentives hold (v1.0)

Founder direction (2026-10-01), in the founders' words:

- *Is this model sustainable in Tanzania? Incentives all aligned?*
- *Write prompt and response to map out all stakeholders (what they are
  made of and why, their mission and incentives etc.) and work backward
  from the problem to the solution, to make sure our build isn't an idea
  looking for a problem, but a full understanding of the problem and then
  building the solution.*

As in Prompts B–I: the response first (Part 1), the build prompt after
(Part 2), then the decisions (Part 3), with sources at the end.

Every claim carries one of three labels, so nobody mistakes a belief for a
fact:

- **Known**: published, with a source in the list at the end.
- **Reported**: heard from partners or the field; not yet checked.
- **Assumed**: our belief; to be tested in the pilot (§1.9).

---

## Part 1 — Response

### 1.1 The short answer

**Is it sustainable?** Not proven yet. It can be, under conditions that are
clear and testable. Today the app is well built for a problem we have
mostly *reasoned about*, not yet *measured*. That is the honest risk behind
your question.

**Are the incentives aligned?** Within a single sale, mostly yes:

- nobody is paid on a promise;
- every hand-over is proven;
- prices are fixed and public per area;
- two people approve anything risky.

Across the whole system, **not yet**. There are seven places where what one
party earns works against what another needs (§1.6). The worst four:

1. Delivery pay ignores distance, so far villages lose out.
2. Sellers and suppliers earn more from disposables, while girls, budgets
   and the environment may be better served by reusables.
3. Free donor giveaways undercut the paid local sellers the system depends
   on.
4. Nobody has decided who pays Dandelion itself. A fee on each pack would
   raise the price for the poorest buyers.

**What would make it sustainable** (each explained below):

- far villages served by a **monthly bundled run**, paid for openly, never
  by the girl;
- local sellers earning from a **basket of products**, not pads alone;
- donor money entering **as vouchers spent at local sellers**, not as
  giveaways;
- **organisations, donors and manufacturers paying Dandelion** for proven
  delivery, never the consumer;
- **stock on trust** for people with a clean record, so cash is not needed
  up front;
- a pilot that **measures** all of this before more features are built.

**The recommendation:** pause new features for 6–8 weeks. Run the field
discovery and a small pilot (§1.10). Build only what the pilot shows is
needed, plus a page that measures it (J1).

### 1.2 Start from the outcome, not the app

The outcome we want, in one sentence:

> Every month, a girl or woman — including in a far village — has the
> menstrual product she prefers, when she needs it, at a price her household
> can manage, with privacy and dignity; and everyone who gets it to her earns
> enough to keep doing it, without a donor paying for every pack forever.

The sentence has two halves: **her outcome** and **the system staying
alive**. A model that serves her with grants until the grants stop fails the
second half. A model that earns well by serving only towns fails the first.

Three lessons from published evidence shape what Dandelion should promise:

1. **Don't sell Dandelion on "girls miss school".**
   - In Nepal, girls missed about 0.4 days a school year because of their
     period, and cups made no measurable difference (Oster & Thornton).
   - In western Kenya, cups and pads did not reduce dropout. They were
     linked to **lower sexually transmitted infection risk**
     (Phillips-Howard et al., 2016).

   The strong, defensible case is **dignity, health, comfort, cost and
   reliability**. Attendance is a hoped-for side effect, not the headline.

2. **Help upstream doesn't reach girls unless the last link is visible.**
   Tanzania removed VAT on sanitary pads in July 2018. Prices did not fall,
   and VAT was put back in July 2019 (Known: The Citizen; The EastAfrican;
   IMHER). Dandelion's fixed, public, per-area prices can show whether a tax
   cut or subsidy reaches the girl. That's evidence policymakers lack.

3. **Last-mile retail to scattered small buyers burns cash.** Copia, a
   Kenyan rural e-commerce company with agents and depots that raised about
   USD 123 million, entered administration in May 2024. A Kenyan court
   ordered its liquidation in September 2026 (Known: TechCabal; WeeTracker).
   Small orders per person could not pay for warehouses, agents and
   delivery. Dandelion must keep fixed costs tiny, bundle orders, and grow
   one district at a time.

### 1.5 Every stakeholder: who they are, what they want, where it breaks

For each stakeholder: who they are and what they're made of (people,
money, constraints); what they want (their mission or goal); what they fear
or lose; what Dandelion gives them; where the incentive can break; and the
condition under which they stay.

#### 1. Girls and women (the customers)

- **Who they are.** Three groups with different lives:
  - in-school girls aged about 10–19: usually no phone of their own, little
    or no cash, decisions shared with a mother or guardian;
  - out-of-school young women: some cash, often a shared phone;
  - adult women: buy for themselves and their daughters.
  - Rural or town matters more than age: cloth is more common in villages
    (Known).
- **What they want.** A product that works and that they trust, available
  when needed, at a price that fits, with privacy. Fewer leaks, less worry,
  no shame.
- **What they fear.** Being seen or talked about; paying and not receiving;
  products that cause irritation; being pressured.
- **What Dandelion gives them.**
  - texts only, no app needed;
  - pay in parts with no interest and no fees from us;
  - a price fixed on the day she starts;
  - a hand-over code, so nobody else can collect her product;
  - a receipt that doesn't name the product.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Instalments look kind, but each mobile-money payment can carry a fee
    (§1.6, M6).
  - A shared phone means a text can be read by someone else (Assumed; test).
  - A girl may not control the money at all.
- **They stay if** it's always available, private and no dearer than the
  shop. Measure **repeat purchase in months 2 and 3**: it's the single best
  test of whether the model is solving a real problem.

#### 2. Mothers, fathers, guardians, husbands

- **Who they are.** The people who usually hold the household's cash and
  can allow or block a purchase. Their attitudes vary widely.
- **What they want.** To spend little, to protect the girl's reputation,
  and to keep to cultural and religious norms.
- **What they fear.** Products seen as inappropriate (some reusable or
  insertable products); being judged; wasted money.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** A known local woman as seller, a fixed
  price, and paying in parts in step with their income.
- **Where it breaks.** If they are not convinced, demand never shows up in
  any data. That's why they appear here although the app never names them.
- **They stay if** the local seller and the school talk to them. Education
  must include parents, not only girls.

#### 3. Local sellers (field champions)

- **Who they are.** A woman from the community: often already a small
  trader, a community health volunteer, or a teacher's contact. She has a
  basic smartphone or a shared one, little spare cash, and limited time,
  because this is usually a side income.
- **What they want.** Income, and respect in the community for helping.
- **What they fear.**
  - Paying for stock that does not sell.
  - Customers who stop paying mid-plan.
  - Travel to the hub eating her margin.
  - Being blamed for a bad product.
- **What Dandelion gives them.**
  - earnings shown on her home screen;
  - stock she can see before she starts a plan;
  - restock requests;
  - protection from disputes (codes, receipts);
  - training content.
- **Where it breaks.**
  1. **Pads alone are a side income, not a living** (§1.7).
  2. She earns monthly from disposables, but only once every 1–3 years
     from a reusable kit (M2).
  3. Free giveaways in her village take her customers (M3).
  4. Per-sale pay could tempt pressure selling. The app bans pressure
     language and requires consent; complaints must be watched.
- **She stays if** her net earnings after travel and time beat her other
  options. That probably needs a basket of products: in Uganda, Living Goods
  health promoters earn a 15–20% margin on a basket of health and household
  goods (Known).

#### 4. Hub keepers

- **Who they are.** Usually an existing shopkeeper (a *duka*) or pharmacy in
  a ward centre, sometimes also a mobile-money agent. They have a lockable
  room, opening hours, foot traffic, and some working capital.
- **What they want.** More customers through the door, a margin on stock
  that moves, and a reliable supplier.
- **What they fear.** Stock that sits; theft; damaged deliveries they get
  blamed for; tying up cash.
- **What Dandelion gives them.**
  - inspection with a checklist and a delivery code, so they never accept
    bad stock blind;
  - restock suggestions that read their sales, road and rains (Prompt I);
  - two-admin resolution of problems.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - They pay the delivery partner up front (M4).
  - Their own shop could sell directly and take the local sellers' trade.
    Sale paths are per area and need two admins; watch the mix.
- **They stay if** the stock moves and their cash isn't tied up.
  Stock on trust (Prompt I, I7) helps most here.

#### 5. Delivery partners (boda-boda riders)

- **Who they are.** Mostly young men. Many ride a bike owned by someone else
  and owe that owner TZS 8,000–10,000 every day (Known). They pay for their
  own fuel and repairs, live day to day, and are regulated by LATRA.
- **What they want.** The best income per hour; predictable work; not being
  cheated.
- **What they fear.** Long trips that pay less than passengers would; mud
  and breakdowns; carrying cash or stock they could be blamed for losing;
  police stops.
- **What Dandelion gives them.**
  - payment confirmed before they pick up;
  - a delivery code at the hub;
  - earnings on their home screen;
  - nobody can claim they lost stock without the custody record.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Pay is per pack, not per trip, so far villages lose out (M1; Prompt I,
    I4).
  - They pay the factory up front (M4).
  - Safeguarding: they should not be the people handing products to girls
    (§3.5).
- **They stay if** a trip pays at least what a day of passengers would, and
  they know the pay before saying yes.

#### 6. Suppliers and manufacturers

- **Who they are.** Three kinds:
  - local converters making disposable pads: machines, imported raw
    materials, sensitive to tax and the exchange rate;
  - makers of reusable pads, often sewing groups or social enterprises;
  - importers and wholesalers, often in Dar es Salaam's Kariakoo market.
- **What they want.** Volume, predictable orders, fast payment, a brand
  people trust.
- **What they fear.**
  - Irregular orders.
  - Late payment.
  - Being undercut by imports or counterfeits.
  - Tax changes. In 2019 domestic factories cut production when the VAT
    exemption left them unable to reclaim input VAT (Known).
- **What Dandelion gives them.**
  - paid before release;
  - demand they can see per hub;
  - quality tracking;
  - lead times recorded honestly.
- **Where it breaks.** Disposable makers prefer monthly repeat sales (M2).
  A supplier that sells directly to organisations can bypass the chain
  (allowed per area by two admins).
- **They stay if** Dandelion's orders are regular and paid fast. Showing
  them the demand forecast is a strong pull for them.

#### 7. Schools

- **Who they are.**
  - **Public schools:** basic education has been fee-free since 2016, so
    their budgets are thin.
  - **Staff:** a head teacher, and often a teacher assigned to girls'
    welfare.
  - **Facilities:** often missing (C9).
  - **Rules:** the national school WASH guideline (2016) sets the standard.
- **What they want.** Girls in class and comfortable; no scandal; support
  that doesn't create work.
- **What they fear.** Extra administration; parents' objections; being
  blamed for privacy failures.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** Bulk delivery with a receipt and a public
  record. The school buys or receives as an organisation, and no data about
  individual girls is kept.
- **Where it breaks.** Most schools cannot pay. Their supply depends on an
  NGO or donor, which is why vouchers (M3) and paid delivery (M5) matter.
- **They stay if** it costs them no money and little time.

#### 8. NGOs and community organisations

- **Who they are.** From village community-based organisations to
  international NGOs. They run on project funding cycles of 1–3 years,
  report to donors, and have staff with targets.
- **What they want.** Impact they can show, cost per girl reached, smooth
  programme delivery, renewed funding.
- **What they fear.** Funding cuts (they are happening); scandals over lost
  stock; reports that cannot be backed up.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** Proof at the last link: custody, codes,
  receipts and a public record. One supply line instead of their own
  logistics.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Their incentive is big numbers. "Packs distributed" overstates impact,
    while **girl-months covered** is honest (M7).
  - Free giveaways crowd out sellers (M3).
- **They stay if** Dandelion makes their reporting easier and cheaper than
  doing logistics themselves. That is something they would pay for (M5).

#### 9. Donors and funders

- **Who they are.** Bilateral and multilateral agencies (for example UNICEF
  and UNFPA), private foundations, company giving programmes (banks,
  telecoms, mining), and diaspora giving.
- **What they want.** Verified outcomes, low overhead, a story, and an exit
  plan.
- **What they fear.** Fraud; dependency; funding a pilot that dies when the
  grant ends.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** Proof per unit; a choice of where their
  money goes (a school, a route, vouchers); and a model that gets cheaper as
  it grows.
- **Where it breaks.** Donor priorities shift; US aid was suspended in
  January 2025 (Known). A model that needs donors for every pack is fragile.
- **They stay if** their money **unlocks** a market rather than replacing
  it: vouchers, route sponsorship, stock on trust.

#### 10. Government

- **Who.**
  - **National ministries:** Health; Education; Community Development and
    Gender; and regional and local government (PO-RALG / TAMISEMI).
  - **Regulators:**
    - the Tanzania Revenue Authority (TRA): VAT and duties;
    - the Tanzania Bureau of Standards (TBS): product standards;
    - the Bank of Tanzania: payments;
    - the Personal Data Protection Commission;
    - LATRA: transport.
  - **Local:** district councils and their community development officers,
    and ward and village leaders.
- **What they want.** Girls healthy and in school; tax revenue; order;
  credit for progress.
- **What they fear.** Unregistered activity; data scandals; foreign-run
  schemes that bypass them.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** Aggregate, anonymous evidence on price
  pass-through and coverage; legal, registered operation.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - Tax swings move prices (2018–2019).
  - Collecting and paying out other people's money may need a payments
    licence (Prompt I, decision §3.5).
- **They help if** they are involved early. A district partner opens doors
  to schools and village leaders.

#### 11. Mobile-money providers and agents

- **Who.** M-Pesa (Vodacom), Airtel Money, Mixx by Yas (formerly Tigo Pesa,
  renamed in November 2024), HaloPesa, and their agents.
- **What they want.** Transaction volume, merchant growth, and good public
  relations.
- **What they fear.** Fraud; rules imposed by regulators.
- **What Dandelion gives them.** Steady merchant payments.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - **Fees and taxes change behaviour.** After the July 2021 levy on
    mobile-money transactions, use dropped sharply and the levy was cut by
    30% within weeks (Known).
  - Paying a merchant's "Lipa" number is generally free for the payer
    (Known: Vodacom FAQ), but rules differ by provider and network.
  - Each hop in a chain of payments can carry a fee (M6).
- **They help if** every payment in the chain goes to a merchant till, or
  the platform collects once (G1).

#### 12. Community health workers and clinics

- **Who.** Community health workers and dispensaries, already trusted on
  health topics.
- **What they want.** Fewer infections; healthy girls; to be paid for their
  work.
- **Where they fit.** Education, referral for pain or infection, and
  possibly acting as local sellers. That is the Living Goods model, and it
  needs care so a health worker's advice is never a sales pitch.

#### 13. The existing market (shops, pharmacies, wholesalers, cloth)

- **Who.** Town shops and pharmacies already sell disposables. Wholesalers
  in Dar es Salaam supply them. Cloth and other home-made options are the
  real competitor in villages (Known).
- **What this means.** Dandelion is not inventing a market; it is trying to
  extend a working town market to places it doesn't reach. A village shop
  that starts stocking pads because Dandelion made it possible is a
  success, not a competitor.

#### 14. Dandelion itself

- **Who.** The founders, a small team, the software, and its running costs:
  text messages per customer, hosting, staff, and field visits.
- **What it wants.** The outcome in §1.2, and to survive long enough to
  reach it.
- **What it fears.** Running out of money (Copia); being seen as a
  middleman taking a cut from poor girls; a data or safeguarding scandal.
- **Where it breaks.**
  - **Nobody has decided who pays Dandelion** (M5).
  - Every feature that isn't tied to a condition in §1.3 costs money and
    attention (§1.8).
- **It survives if** organisations, donors and manufacturers pay for proven
  delivery and the platform's costs stay small. It must never sell or share
  personal data.

### 1.6 Where the incentives line up, and where they break

**Lined up (keep):**
- money moves only after the provider confirms it;
- stock moves only when both sides confirm it;
- prices are fixed per area and shown on receipts;
- two admins approve anything that decides who earns;
- restocking follows demand, now including the road;
- the customer's product is never named in a text.

**Broken or at risk:**

| # | Misalignment | Who wins, who loses | Fix | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M1 | Per-pack pay ignores distance | Riders win on easy trips; far villages lose | Trip pay by road and season, shown before accepting | Prompt I, I4 (decision §3.1–3.2) |
| M2 | Disposables pay sellers and suppliers monthly; reusables pay once every 1–3 years | Sellers prefer disposables; girls on a budget, donors and the environment may prefer reusables | A one-off margin on reusables that reflects the lost repeat sales, plus paid follow-up visits at 1 and 3 months (donor-funded); accessories (soap, carry bags); measure what girls choose | Decision §3.3 |
| M3 | Free giveaways vs paid local sellers | The donor's report wins; the seller loses customers and quits; the girl loses supply when the grant ends | **Vouchers, not giveaways:** the donor pays, the girl redeems at her local seller, the seller earns her normal margin, the donor gets proof per redemption | Decision §3.2; build J3 |
| M4 | Cash up front at every link | Whoever has cash; the chain stalls in villages | Platform collects once and pays out (G1); stock on trust | Prompt I, I6–I7 |
| M5 | Who pays Dandelion? | A per-pack platform fee would make the poorest pay for the system | **Business-to-business:** organisations and donors pay per verified delivery; manufacturers pay a distribution fee; consumers never pay a platform fee | Decision §3.1 |
| M6 | Fees stack at each hop and each instalment | Mobile-money providers; the payer loses a little each time | Pay merchant tills (usually free to the payer); fewer hops (G1); count fees in the pilot measures | J1, J5 |
| M7 | "Packs distributed" inflates impact | Reports look bigger; funders are misled | Report **girl-months covered** (a disposable pack ≈ 1 month, a reusable kit ≈ many months; parameters) | J1, J7 |

Four more to watch:
- **Per-sale pay can tempt pressure selling.** Consent and banned pressure
  language are already built; watch complaints.
- **A hub's own shop can compete with local sellers.** Sale paths need two
  admins; watch the mix of sales.
- **Personal data must never become a revenue line.** Write that into the
  privacy policy.
- **A tax cut or subsidy may not reach the girl.** Publish aggregate prices
  so it's visible whether it does.

### 1.7 The arithmetic that decides it (illustrative — replace with pilot data)

Every number in this section is **Assumed** and is there to show the shape
of the problem, not to plan on. The pilot measures the real ones.

**The far trip.**
- **The trip:** a 95 km dirt road, 190 km there and back. Fuel, the rider's
  day (he must clear his TZS 8,000–10,000 to the bike's owner and still
  earn), and wear might come to **TZS 40,000–60,000** all in.
- **Cost per pack** at TZS 50,000 a trip:

| Packs on the trip | Delivery cost per pack |
| --- | --- |
| 20 (one seller's restock) | TZS 2,500 |
| 100 (a village's month) | TZS 500 |
| 300 (several villages and a school) | TZS 170 |

- **The comparison:** a woman or girl spends about TZS 3,500 a month on
  pads (Known).
- **Lesson:** a far village is sustainable only with a **bundled monthly
  run**: every seller's and school's order on one trip, on a schedule
  (Prompt I, I5). Otherwise someone pays for the gap. On-demand delivery of
  single orders to scattered villages is the Copia trap.

**The local seller.**
- Suppose a margin of TZS 300–500 a pack and 40 regular customers.
- That's TZS 12,000–20,000 a month: useful extra income, but only one to
  two days of what a boda rider pays the bike's owner.
- **Lesson:** to be a living, the seller role needs a **basket of products**
  (soap, underwear, pain relief where allowed, other health and household
  goods). That also argues for building the app to handle **any product**
  (Prompt I, I10) — for the sake of sellers' incomes, not as a feature.

**Dandelion.**
- Costs: text messages (several per sale), hosting, a few staff, field
  visits.
- With no consumer fee, these must come from organisations, donors and
  manufacturers (M5).
- **The test:** in the pilot, cost per girl-month covered (delivery +
  margins + Dandelion's costs) against what funders pay today for the same
  coverage.

### 1.9 The assumptions to test, riskiest first

| # | Assumption | Why it matters | Test in 6–8 weeks | What would change our plan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Rural girls and women will **buy again** every month if it's nearby | Without repeat purchase there is no model | Pilot sales in 2 villages and 1 town; repeat rate in months 2–3 | Below about a third repeating → rethink the product, price or channel before scaling |
| A2 | Organisations and donors will **pay for proven delivery** | Funds Dandelion and the far miles | 3 paid pilots or signed letters of intent | No payer → Dandelion has no business model yet |
| A3 | A far village's month fits **one bundled run** (around 100+ packs) | Decides delivery cost per pack | Count the orders per run | Too few → combine villages, add schools, or sponsor the route |
| A4 | Sellers can earn **enough with a basket** | Sellers stay | Net earnings after travel and time | Too little → a broader basket or a different seller profile |
| A5 | Households **want instalments** and can pay without fees | Instalments are a large part of the build | Offer both options; record fees | Most pay once → simplify |
| A6 | Donors **prefer vouchers with proof** to giveaways | Protects sellers (M3) | 2 donor conversations with a voucher offer | They insist on giveaways → route them through sellers anyway, with a handling fee |
| A7 | Texts to **shared phones** are acceptable | Privacy (C4) | Interviews; complaints | Not acceptable → a code-only receipt, or the school as contact |
| A8 | **Counterfeit or poor products** exist locally | Quality as a reason to buy from us | 20 test purchases; check the TBS mark | None found → drop it from the pitch |
| A9 | **Reusables** are accepted where water allows | Cost and environment | Pilot in an area with confirmed water; 1- and 3-month follow-up | Low use → focus on disposables plus education |

### 1.10 The field discovery plan (6–8 weeks, before more features)

**Who to talk to** (with consent; girls under 18 only through a school or
NGO with a safeguarding protocol and a guardian's consent):

| Group | How many |
| --- | --- |
| Girls and women | 20 |
| Mothers | 10 |
| Teachers | 5 |
| Shopkeepers who could be hubs | 8 |
| Boda-boda riders | 8 |
| Suppliers | 3 |
| NGOs | 3 |
| Donors | 2 |
| The district community development officer | 1 |

**What to ask** (a few each, open questions, no leading):

- **Girls and women:**
  - What did you use last month, where did it come from, and what did it
    cost?
  - What stops you using what you would prefer?
  - Who decides, and who pays?
  - Would a text from a seller be all right on your phone?
- **Mothers:** When money is short, what happens to pads? Would paying in
  parts help, or not?
- **Shopkeepers:** Why do or don't you stock pads? How long does a pack sit
  on the shelf? What would make you stock them?
- **Riders:** What does a day earn? What would a 95 km dirt trip have to pay
  for you to take it? In the rains?
- **NGOs and donors:**
  - What do you pay today per girl reached?
  - What proof do you need?
  - Would you pay for proven delivery?
  - Would you fund vouchers instead of giveaways?

**The pilot:** 2 villages (one far, on dirt) and 1 town, run with the app as
it is, with paper as a fallback.

**Measures**, from the app where it already records them (J1):
- girl-months covered;
- repeat purchase rate in months 2 and 3;
- instalment plans completed or stalled;
- days out of stock per hub;
- delivery cost per pack by road type;
- seller net earnings per month;
- rider pay per trip against a day of passengers;
- fees per pack;
- complaints and safeguarding reports;
- the share of packs funded by donors.

**Decide in advance what counts as failure.** Write down now the numbers at
which you will change course (§3.8), so the pilot can't be read
optimistically afterwards.

### 1.11 Verdict

**Sustainable if:**
- far villages are served by bundled, scheduled runs paid for openly;
- sellers earn from a basket of products;
- organisations, donors and manufacturers pay Dandelion, never the consumer;
- donor money enters as vouchers through the same sellers;
- stock is on trust for those with a clean record;
- the pilot's numbers say so.

**Not sustainable if:**
- delivery goes order by order to scattered villages;
- giveaways empty the market;
- a platform fee lands on the poorest buyer;
- it depends on one donor;
- more features get built before the field has answered §1.9.

## Part 2 — The prompt: what to build next, in order

Recommendation: build **J1 only**, now, and freeze other new features until
the field discovery (§1.10) reports. J2–J7 wait for decisions and evidence.

### J1 — Pilot measures (no decision needed)

An admin-only page, **Is it working?**, built from data the app already
records. All figures are aggregated per area, with no names:
- **Girl-months covered:** units handed over × months per product type.
  The months are a parameter per product, approved by two admins.
- **Repeat purchase rate:** customers buying again in months 2 and 3.
- **Plans completed, stalled (no payment for 30 days) and refunded.**
- **Days out of stock per hub and product** (`hub_stock_days`).
- **Delivered cost per pack by road type:** margins today; trip pay once I4
  exists.
- **Seller net earnings per month:** confirmed money received minus money
  paid. Optional self-reported travel cost.
- **Fees per pack:** where the provider reports them.
- **Donor-funded share of units.**
- **Complaints and exceptions per 100 units.**

Tests: each figure checked on a seeded dataset with known answers. The page
never shows a person's name or phone. It is admin-only.

### J2 — A confidential way for a girl to report a problem (after §3.5)

- **How she reports:** a text keyword (for example *MSAADA*, "help")
  reaches a named safeguarding lead (two people), never the seller.
- **What she gets back:** an acknowledgement text with no details.
- **How it's recorded:** a case opened in the problems inbox under its own
  type, visible only to the safeguarding leads.
- **Overlap:** this shares its mechanics with Prompt C §5.3 (customer
  keywords), which is still waiting on a decision.

### J3 — Vouchers (after §3.2)

- **Buying:** a donor or organisation buys vouchers for an area.
- **Giving:** a voucher code is sent by text to a girl enrolled through a
  school or NGO, with consent.
- **Redeeming:** she redeems it at her local seller. The seller earns her
  normal margin, and the donor sees proof of each redemption.
- **Expiry:** an unredeemed voucher expires back to the funder.
- **Data:** no personal data reaches the funder.

### J4 — A basket of products (after §3.4)

- Several product types per seller, with checks and education per type
  (Prompt I, I10).
- Earnings shown per type.

### J5 — Fee tracking

- Record the mobile-money fee per payment where the provider reports it;
  use a provider tariff table otherwise.
- Fees show in J1.

### J6 — Follow-up visits after a reusable sale (after §3.3)

- A task for the seller at 1 and 3 months after selling a reusable kit
  ("is it working, any problems?"), paid from a donor budget.
- Outcomes recorded without health details.

### J7 — Reports for organisations and funders

- Girl-months covered, proof of delivery and price pass-through per area,
  as a downloadable report.
- Aggregated only.

## Part 3 — Founders' decisions

| # | Decision | Recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3.1 | **Who pays Dandelion** | Business-to-business. Organisations and donors pay per verified delivery or girl-month; manufacturers pay a distribution fee. **Never** a platform fee on the consumer. |
| 3.2 | **Vouchers or giveaways** | Vouchers redeemed at local sellers. When a funder insists on free distribution, route it through sellers with a handling fee, so the market survives the grant. |
| 3.3 | **Disposables and reusables** | Offer both where water allows. Pay sellers fairly for reusables: a one-off margin that reflects the lost repeat sales, plus paid follow-up visits. Let the pilot show what girls choose. |
| 3.4 | **The seller's basket** | Pick 3–5 products the community already buys (soap, underwear, other hygiene items). Avoid medicines unless licensed. |
| 3.5 | **Safeguarding** | Name two safeguarding leads. Build the confidential reporting channel (J2). Keep **village drops** (a delivery partner selling directly to customers) **off** in any area where girls under 18 are served; hand-overs to girls go through women sellers or schools. Write a code of conduct for sellers, riders and staff. |
| 3.6 | **Feature freeze** | Yes: 6–8 weeks of field discovery and pilot; build only J1 meanwhile. |
| 3.7 | **First segment** | Both in one district, measured separately: schools and NGOs (business-to-business, predictable volume that pays for the bundled run) and women buying from sellers. The organisations' volume is what makes the far run affordable for the individual buyers. |
| 3.8 | **Thresholds to change course** | Set them now. For example: repeat purchase below about a third in month 3; far-route delivery cost per pack above an agreed share of the price after bundling; seller net earnings below an agreed floor; no paying organisation by week 8. |
